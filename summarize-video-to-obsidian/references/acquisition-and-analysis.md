# Acquisition and analysis

## Evidence ladder

Use the richest reliable source available, and record which source was used.

1. **Manual subtitles:** Usually the best text evidence, but still check names, numbers, code, and timing against the video when important.
2. **Automatic captions:** Useful for structure and broad meaning. Treat proper nouns, homophones, punctuation, formulas, and mixed-language passages as uncertain.
3. **Audio transcription:** Preserve timestamps and speaker changes when the transcription system supports them. Inspect low-confidence or high-impact passages against the audio.
4. **Audiovisual inspection:** Use when no transcript exists or when the video's meaning depends on visuals. Never describe the full video as reviewed after inspecting only the opening or a few frames.

The video page metadata is provenance and context, not a substitute for the content. Titles and descriptions may be incomplete, promotional, or written by a reuploader.

## Subtitle selection

Prefer a manual track in the user's requested language. Otherwise choose:

1. a manual track in the conversation language;
2. a manual track in the original spoken language;
3. an automatic track in the same order;
4. translated captions only when the user needs that language and no stronger source exists.

Keep a note of whether captions are manual, automatic, or translated. Do not silently present automatic or translated captions as exact words from the speaker.

## Content reconstruction

Build a short outline before summarizing:

- What question or problem is the video addressing?
- What is the main claim or teaching objective?
- How does the speaker divide the topic?
- What evidence, demonstrations, examples, or counterarguments support each section?
- What conclusion, recommendation, or unresolved question remains?

For interviews or debates, preserve who said what and where participants disagree. For tutorials, capture prerequisites, ordered steps, commands, expected outcomes, and failure modes. For lectures, preserve definitions, derivations, examples, and conceptual dependencies. For reviews, distinguish observed features from preference and recommendation.

## Visual inspection

Visuals are mandatory evidence when the transcript refers to information that is not spoken aloud. Inspect:

- title cards and agenda slides for structure;
- diagrams, equations, tables, and charts for relationships and quantities;
- code and terminal output for exact syntax and results;
- product interfaces and demonstrations for state changes;
- labels, warnings, citations, and on-screen lists;
- before/after frames for claimed visual changes.

Sample around chapter boundaries and transcript cues such as “as shown,” “this chart,” or “look here.” Add more samples around fast demonstrations. When a detail remains unreadable, omit it or mark it as unclear.

## Accuracy rules

- A timestamp is evidence only if it comes from a subtitle cue, chapter marker, or direct playback inspection.
- A direct quote must be verified against manual subtitles or the audio. Automatic captions alone justify a paraphrase unless the wording is checked.
- Attribute claims: “The speaker argues…” is different from “Research shows…”.
- Preserve hedges such as “may,” “usually,” and “in this example.”
- Do not add background knowledge to the source summary. Put requested research or fact-checking in a separately labeled section with citations.
- If only part of the video was accessible, state the exact coverage and do not summarize the inaccessible remainder.

## Long videos

For long content, process by chapters or stable time windows and keep a running outline. Summarize each segment independently, then reconcile repeated or revised claims before producing the final note. Do not truncate the transcript and infer the ending.
